Famous Elephants of Sri Lanka

Elephants have held important places in Sri Lankan history, religious ceremonies, royal traditions and public memory. This page introduces several well-known elephants and explains the difference between documented modern lives and older historical or literary traditions.

Raja of the Temple of the Tooth · Wikimedia Commons image search

Raja – the Maligawa Tusker

Raja (c. 1913–1988) became one of Sri Lanka's best-known ceremonial elephants. He was associated with the Sri Dalada Maligawa in Kandy and carried the sacred casket during the Esala Perahera for many years. His calm public presence made him a much-loved symbol of the procession.

After his death, Raja was honoured nationally and his preserved body is displayed at the National Museum in Kandy. His story is also a reminder of the long-standing place of elephants in Sri Lankan ceremonial culture.

Nadungamuwe Raja · Wikimedia Commons image search

Nadungamuwe Raja

Nadungamuwe Raja (c. 1953–2022) was a famous Sri Lankan tusker known for carrying the sacred casket at the Kandy Esala Perahera. He was widely recognised for his size, tusks and carefully managed ceremonial journeys. His death in 2022 prompted widespread public tributes.

His story is part of recent Sri Lankan cultural memory. Ceremonial elephants require specialist care, veterinary supervision and careful attention to welfare and safety.

Millangoda Raja · Wikimedia Commons image search

Millangoda Raja

Millangoda Raja was another celebrated Sri Lankan tusker remembered for his impressive tusks and association with the Kandy Esala Perahera. He became part of the public memory of the island's ceremonial elephant tradition.

Accounts and photographs of famous tuskers help document how individual animals became recognisable cultural figures. Historical descriptions should be checked against reliable records because names and details can sometimes be repeated inaccurately.

Deega Danthu 1 · Wikimedia Commons image search

Deega Danthu 1

Deega Danthu 1 is remembered as a prominent Sri Lankan tusker and a participant in the country's ceremonial elephant tradition. He is often mentioned among notable elephants associated with public religious pageants.

Individual elephant histories can be difficult to verify from short online summaries. For exact dates, ownership and event records, consult contemporary reporting and official cultural or wildlife sources.

Kandy Esala Perahera · Wikimedia Commons

Elephants of the Esala Perahera

The Kandy Esala Perahera is a major annual cultural and religious procession. Historically, elephants have taken part in ceremonial roles, including carrying ornamental structures and the casket associated with the Sacred Tooth Relic. Not every elephant in a procession is a tusker, and the role of carrying the casket is a special one.

Modern discussions increasingly include animal welfare, safe handling, veterinary care and whether ceremonial practices can evolve while respecting cultural heritage.

Elephants and the royal courts

Elephants were important in South Asian kingdoms for transport, warfare, diplomacy, royal display and ceremonial life. A royal elephant could represent strength, prestige and authority. Their use varied by period and kingdom, and not every story about a royal elephant is independently documented.

In Sri Lankan chronicles and historical traditions, elephants appear in accounts of kings, battles and royal processions. These stories should be read with attention to the difference between literary tradition, inscriptional evidence and modern historical interpretation.

Kandula – the elephant of King Dutugemunu

Illustrative Sri Lankan elephant; not a verified portrait of the historical Kandula.

Kandula is the famous elephant associated in the Sri Lankan chronicle tradition with King Dutugemunu (Dutthagamani). In the Mahavamsa, Kandula is described as the king's powerful war elephant and an important companion in the campaign against King Elara.

The story of Kandula is deeply embedded in Sri Lankan historical memory. However, there is no modern photograph or scientifically verified physical portrait of an elephant from that ancient period. Images on this page are therefore illustrative, not claimed to depict Kandula himself.

Why the story matters

Kandula illustrates the historical and symbolic importance of elephants in ancient Sri Lanka. The account is valuable as a chronicle tradition, while details should be interpreted in the context of the text's date, purpose and historical setting.
